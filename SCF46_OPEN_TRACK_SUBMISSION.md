## 1. Products & Services

Crossing Protocol brings issuer-led, uniform-price auctions to private-market real-world assets on Stellar, including private credit, real estate interests and structured notes. Its open-source Soroban contracts let issuers sell tokenized assets or buy them back in scheduled auctions, while eligible investors and holders submit funded orders with their own price limits. Crossing gathers participation over a defined window and allocates accepted orders at one clearing price, without requiring a continuously funded market-making pool.

Private-market assets can have interested buyers and sellers without enough trading activity to sustain a continuous market. Holders may need liquidity before maturity, and issuers may want to distribute inventory or reduce outstanding supply. AMMs depend on funded pools, order books on executable standing orders, and RFQs on available liquidity providers. Crossing offers a scheduled process for assembling eligible participation and allocating it under public rules. Execution depends on funded investor demand or an issuer-funded buyback budget; an auction organizes available capital rather than guaranteeing liquidity.

The clearest initial use case is a scheduled private-credit buyback. An issuer sets a stablecoin budget and maximum purchase price; authorized holders escrow the tokens they want to sell and specify minimum prices. Lower asks receive priority, and accepted quantities are repriced at the common clearing price without exceeding the budget. Marginal orders share the remaining allocation pro rata, subject to integer rounding. Unused budget and unallocated tokens remain attributable to their owners. Repurchased tokens go to the configured treasury or issuing account; a transfer to the classic issuing account burns them. This gives the issuer a way to manage supply and holders an opportunity to exit within a defined liquidity event.

For a sale, the issuer escrows a fixed quantity of RWA tokens and investors escrow settlement assets against their maximum prices. Higher bids receive priority. Oversubscribed sales clear at the marginal price; undersubscribed sales use the reserve price if the minimum-fill condition is met. Funding both sides removes the need to collect a winner's payment after the auction. Each bid resolves independently to fixed recipients; issuer sale proceeds are credited only after the corresponding token delivery. Anyone can trigger a claim, while withdrawal requires the credit owner's authorization. Asset restrictions and failed transfers can still delay delivery or withdrawal.

Issuers choose open bidding, with public price-level totals, or sealed bidding. The PoC implements sealed commitments followed by bidder-authorized reveals. The proposed product adds automatic revelation: the client encrypts bid details for a threshold decryption service and records a commitment alongside the funded deposit. After bidding ends, reveal workers would decrypt the payloads and publish them during the reveal window. A planned contract extension would permit any caller to reveal the exact committed bid, with timing, hash and collateral checks enforced on-chain. This requires a new contract release and off-chain service; existing auctions retain their original reveal rules. Confidentiality depends on the selected scheme and operator assumptions; addresses and deposits remain visible. Bidder-held recovery material supports manual fallback. Unrevealed bids are excluded from clearing and receive refund credits, subject to an optional penalty fixed at creation. The current cleared-auction penalty defaults to zero and is capped at 50%; cancellation waives it.

Stellar's existing asset infrastructure makes this workflow particularly relevant. Both assets are handled through their Stellar Asset Contracts, so contracts can use issued RWAs and stablecoins without a bridge or replacement token. The current release supports seven-decimal issued assets; XLM pays network costs rather than serving as an auction asset. Creation requires the current RWA SAC administrator's authorization. Permissions on both assets are checked at bidding, for every price-contributing bid during atomic finalization, and again at delivery. Revoked contributors are removed and clearing restarts; frozen refund credits retain their original ownership. The issuer maintains its existing asset permissions, while investor onboarding and legal eligibility remain its responsibility. Freeze and clawback powers remain external asset risks.

The contract architecture comprises a factory, an escrow per auction, a shared price registry and a read-only adapter exposing part of SEP-40. Tick aggregation and bounded closing transactions manage computation; the initial release limits each auction to 16 bids to preserve atomic final authorization checks. Anyone can publish a verified clearing result and its separate execution summary to the registry. Publication can be retried independently of settlement. These records make price, allocated volume and actual delivery visible to other applications, with source times and asset-pair identity. They provide auction observations; using them for lending or valuation requires additional source-quality and freshness controls.

Uniform-price allocation is established in Treasury auctions, and Gnosis EasyAuction provides a blockchain precedent for token sales and buybacks. On Stellar, Octarine's published RFQ approach lets liquidity providers compete to acquire an asset and provide an immediate exit. Crossing's distinction is its scheduled issuer-led sale and budgeted-buyback workflow, combining one clearing price across eligible allocations with native SAC permission checks, individually reserved claims and public execution records. This targets a different operating need from a single RFQ execution.

The proposed next phase adds a TypeScript SDK, issuer and investor dApp, indexed discovery and history, and services for automatic revelation, closing progress and storage retention. Supported wallet flows will be tested end to end; contract state remains authoritative for funds and permissions. Issuer adoption would create funding, order, reveal, closing, delivery and withdrawal transactions using Stellar-issued assets, with XLM paying network fees and storage costs. We will measure completed auctions, independent issuers and participants, actual settlement volume by asset pair and repeat usage. The long-term goal is reusable auction infrastructure that other Stellar applications can integrate, with source and releases remaining under Apache-2.0.

## 2. Traction Evidence

We have built and deployed a Soroban Testnet proof of concept covering the core auction and settlement lifecycle. Its source, tests and documentation are public at [https://github.com/crossing-finance-org/crossing](https://github.com/crossing-finance-org/crossing). The implementation comprises AuctionFactory, Auction, PriceRegistry and OracleAdapter, plus a shared Rust library. It supports funded sales and buybacks, open and commit–reveal bidding, bounded closing, asset-permission checks, independent claims, refunds, expiry, storage maintenance and public price records.

The public transaction log contains 106 Testnet transactions from 24 October 2026 using CRWA and CUSD test assets. All 106 hashes were checked against public Stellar Horizon on 6 November and matched successful transaction records. They cover open and sealed sales, a buyback, authorization-driven exclusion and restarted clearing, withdrawals, price publication and deadline recovery. These are technical demonstrations with test assets, rather than commercial volume or a count of independent customers.

For example, the recorded buyback funded a 20 CUSD budget. Two holders each offered five CRWA at asks of one and two CUSD; both received the common price of two, while a higher ask remained unallocated. A separate sale revoked a bidder's authorization before clearing completed, triggering exclusion and recomputation while preserving the bidder's refund. The log also records a sealed sale with bidder-submitted reveals and a non-reveal penalty applied after the delivery deadline, demonstrating that waiting did not bypass the configured penalty.

Local compiled-WASM tests complement the network demonstrations. An oversubscribed sale of ten RWA units accepts bids for six at nine and eight at seven, clears at seven, and delivers six and four units. Assertions verify refunds of twelve and twenty-eight quote units, seventy quote units earned by the issuer, registry records and fully drained liabilities. A sealed-buyback scenario rejects an altered reveal, executes valid offers at one price and refunds a non-revealer under the zero-penalty configuration.

Fresh local validation passed 60 integration tests against compiled WASM and genuine SACs, nine shared unit tests, 50 design tests and 15 tooling tests. The suite exercises signatures and replay protection, classic multisig, two-leg rollback, frozen credits, clawback deficits, rounding, large-value arithmetic, deployment provenance and recovery. Independent models compare 10,000 generated books; those are model comparisons, not public transactions. Forty-eight reference books also execute against the contracts, and a separate encoder checks canonical commitment vectors.

The 24 October native coverage evidence reports raw executable-line coverage of 97.13% for Auction, 97.53% for AuctionFactory, 98.76% for PriceRegistry and 97.83% for OracleAdapter. Coverage complements the separate WASM behavior tests and is not an external security audit. Local resource tests support the current 16-bid limit. Fresh builds reproduce all four WASM hashes in the Testnet deployment manifest, and its 14 recorded build-input hashes match current source. Together, these results demonstrate core-contract execution while identifying the production qualification still required.

Our team has delivered blockchain, DeFi and tokenized-asset infrastructure, and we have started exploratory discussions with RWA issuers about potential pilots. Those discussions aim to identify a suitable asset, eligible participants, a funded liquidity event and workable operational requirements. Pilot participation and commercial terms remain to be agreed. Crossing's current public delivery evidence is the repository, tests and Testnet history; the next phase builds on that codebase and its concrete release-qualification backlog.

The opportunity is supported by external market research and auction precedent. RedStone Research's 16 October 2026 snapshot reports $3.56 billion of distributed RWAs on Stellar and estimates that 83% of that value has no on-chain market. Zama's January auction announcement reports $118.5 million in commitments, 11,103 bidders and 880 million tokens sold at $0.05, with $44 million paid by winning bids. These examples establish market context and participation in auction mechanisms; issuer pilots will test Crossing's specific fit for illiquid RWAs. The next development phase brings the existing contracts into usable SDK and dApp workflows, adds automatic revelation and indexed history, and qualifies operations and security for deployment. These components remain proposed work; the PoC's sealed mode uses bidder-authorized revelation.

**Five weaknesses, ranked by impact on reviewer confidence, and corrections applied:**

1. **Automatic revelation is a new dependency, not an implemented capability.** The applicant explicitly requested it as proposed scope. The PoC requires the bidder's authorization for `reveal_bid`; Soroban cannot recover bid details from a hash. The application now distinguishes existing commit–reveal from a proposed encrypted-payload and threshold-reveal service. The proposed contract extension permits exact-preimage revelation by another caller within the reveal window, without redirecting recipients or changing committed terms. It requires a new immutable release; existing deployed auctions keep their original authorization rules. The threshold service is a proposed design direction, not a confirmed provider or selected implementation. Bind encrypted payloads and their authenticated context to the network, auction, commitment and release schedule, and verify the recovered preimage through the canonical on-chain commitment. Remaining design work must select the cryptographic scheme, operator threshold, payload availability, authenticated key/share handling, ledger-based release policy and production cost. Early operator collusion can compromise confidentiality; unavailable decryptors or missing payloads can prevent automated reveals. Hash verification prevents accepting altered bids, but does not prove that an encrypted payload was valid or available. User recovery, invalid payloads, partial outages and penalty treatment require explicit acceptance tests. The design must be finalized before claiming the complete application architecture is build-ready.

2. **Market figures and exploratory discussions need different evidence treatment.** The interest form combines external auction results, ecosystem figures, team-controlled transactions and early issuer conversations. The full application now leads with Crossing's own verified PoC, describes the issuer conversations at their reported exploratory stage, and identifies external results as context. No issuer names, agreements, user metrics or committed pilot volumes were added. Before submission, substantiate the discussions with attributable or permissioned evidence, define the issuer problem and establish realistic asset and offering constraints. The blanket assertion that holders have no early-exit option and issuers cannot repurchase tokens was removed: alternatives and redemption rights differ by asset. Issuer funding and legal transferability remain prerequisites.

3. **The reported team experience needs attributable examples.** The applicant states that the team has delivered blockchain, DeFi and tokenized-asset infrastructure. That broad statement is retained as applicant-reported experience, alongside the public Crossing implementation. No named roster, project links, prior award record or role allocation was supplied, so none was invented. The full form should identify contributors, show relevant delivered systems and explain who owns contracts, cryptography, SDK, frontend, operations and issuer integration. Crossing's inspectable work supports domain execution; it does not by itself verify unspecified previous projects or the staffing of the next phase.

4. **The funded delivery boundary and cost are not confirmed.** The full architecture is broader than the current contracts, and automatic revelation adds cryptographic and operational work. Historical proposal budgets and timelines were not carried forward as an approved request. Already completed contracts and Testnet deployment must not be presented as future reimbursable deliverables. A defensible three-stage plan would deliver usable SDK/dApp workflows and contract hardening first, Testnet integration including automatic revelation, threat modeling and monitoring second, and qualified deployment and operational handover third. Each requires named owners, costs, verification and timing. Independent review access is conditional; neither its scheduling nor remediation effort can be assumed free or guaranteed. ## 5. Open Questions

1. **Automatic-reveal architecture and ownership:** Which reviewed threshold-encryption implementation, operator set and threshold will be used? Who owns key generation, encrypted-payload availability, release timing and incident response? Confirm manual fallback and whether a service outage waives penalties. The present contract has no outage-specific waiver; any such policy requires a reviewed change. Complete this design before claiming architecture readiness.

2. **Team evidence:** Which named contributors and completed projects substantiate the reported blockchain, DeFi and tokenized-asset infrastructure experience? Supply public links or permitted evidence, each contributor's role and the next-phase ownership allocation. No previous award or delivered milestone should be attributed without a source.

3. **Pilot discussions:** The applicant reports early discussions with interested issuers. Which counterparties can be named or evidenced, what need did they confirm, and what concrete next step is agreed? Confirm asset identity, transfer constraints, buyback funding, participant count and any consent to disclose the discussion. The first auction must fit the 16-bid release unless a new release is separately qualified; discussions do not establish a committed pilot.

4. **Funded scope and budget:** Confirm the request amount, future work packages, delivery owners, hours, timeline and three costed milestones. Decide whether the initial price deliverable remains the existing raw registry/partial adapter or includes separately scoped curation/full SEP-40. Include a credible route to independent review and remediation without assuming guaranteed Audit Bank access.

5. **Application stage and form:** The interest form is submitted. Has SCF subsequently invited a full Open Track application? If so, confirm the exact deadline, timezone, character limits, attachments and conditions from the invitation and form. Complete the required artifact-origin disclosure, team presentation and other fields. These two narrative sections are not the entire application, and this document has not been submitted on the applicant's behalf.

6. **Eligibility and deployment conditions:** Confirm the applicant/representative, team and jurisdiction eligibility, previous or overlapping funding, and any SCF-specific deployment restrictions. Confirm which production assets and operators can support the proposed launch. The research does not certify legal eligibility or approval to trade a particular RWA.
